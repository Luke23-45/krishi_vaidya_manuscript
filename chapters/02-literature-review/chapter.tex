\chapter{BACKGROUND STUDY AND LITERATURE REVIEW}

\ChapterSection{Background Study}

\ChapterSubsection{Crop Disease Diagnosis as a Decision-Support Problem}

Crop disease diagnosis in smallholder agriculture is not merely an image-classification exercise;
it is a field-level decision-support problem embedded in broader socio-economic and
institutional constraints. When a Nepalese farmer observes discoloration on a rice leaf or
lesions spreading across tomato foliage, the practical question is not ``which class does this
image belong to?'' but rather ``what should I do next, and how urgently?'' Framing the problem
this way---as a decision-support challenge rather than a pattern-recognition task---exposes gaps
that purely model-centric research often overlooks
\parencite{fabregatAgriDSSSurvey2023,desilvaICT4DAgriculture2023}.

The scale of the problem is well documented. The FAO estimates that plant pests and diseases
cause up to 40 percent of annual global crop-production losses, though actual impact varies
with crop, location, management practice, and pathogen pressure
\parencite{faoPlantHealthLosses}. In Nepal specifically, agriculture accounts for approximately
one-quarter of GDP and employs the majority of the rural workforce
\parencite{moaldAgricultureStats2080,nsoAgricultureCensus2021}. The consequences of delayed or
incorrect diagnosis are therefore amplified for smallholder households whose margins for error
are narrow \parencite{pandeyCropLossNepal2022}.

Nepal's movement toward digital agriculture offers a partial infrastructure for addressing
this gap. The FAO's work with the Government of Nepal on a digital agriculture strategy
highlights the need for stronger digital infrastructure, digital literacy, advisory-service
coordination, and rural livelihood integration \parencite{faoNepalDigitalAgriculture}. However,
digital strategies alone do not guarantee that the resulting tools will reach the farmers who
need them most. Research on ICT adoption in Nepalese agriculture identifies persistent
barriers including inconsistent rural connectivity, limited digital literacy among older
farming populations, and insufficient coordination between technology providers and
agricultural extension services \parencite{khatriNepalDigitalAgri2023}. A useful
crop-health system must therefore be designed not only for technical accuracy but also for
offline resilience, linguistic accessibility, and integration with existing agricultural
knowledge systems \parencite{silvestriSmallholderTechAdoption2021}.

\ChapterSection{Literature Review}

\ChapterSubsection{Deep Learning for Plant Disease Detection}

\subsubsection{Dataset evolution and the lab-to-field gap}

The application of deep learning to plant disease detection gained momentum with the PlantVillage
study, which demonstrated that convolutional neural networks could identify 26 diseases across
14 crop species from a curated leaf-image dataset with accuracy exceeding 99 percent
\parencite{mohanty2016plantvillage}. This result established a baseline but also introduced a
methodological concern that subsequent work has repeatedly confirmed: models trained on
laboratory-quality images with uniform backgrounds and controlled lighting do not reliably
generalize to images captured by farmers in real fields
\parencite{singhPlantDoc2020,thakurPlantDiseaseFieldGap2022}.

The PlantDoc dataset addressed this concern directly by collecting ``in-the-wild'' images of 27
disease classes from internet sources, producing a benchmark where even state-of-the-art
classifiers achieved only 60 to 75 percent accuracy under standard transfer-learning protocols
\parencite{singhPlantDoc2020}. This lab-to-field accuracy gap---often exceeding 25 percentage
points---has become a central theme in the field
\parencite{thakurPlantDiseaseFieldGap2022,li2021plantDiseaseReviewCNN}.

Comprehensive reviews of deep-learning approaches for plant disease detection confirm a common
architecture trajectory: from AlexNet and VGGNet through ResNet \parencite{heResNet2016} and
DenseNet to EfficientNet, with transfer learning from ImageNet pre-training as the standard
practice \parencite{saleem2019plantDiseaseML,liu2021plantDiseaseReview}. Performance varies
substantially with dataset quality, class balance, augmentation strategy, and evaluation
protocol. The most informative studies evaluate on field-collected or internet-sourced images
rather than on the original PlantVillage test set, because the latter inflates reported accuracy
relative to practical deployment conditions \parencite{thakurPlantDiseaseFieldGap2022}.

This body of work establishes two points that are directly relevant to Krishi Vaidya. First,
dataset curation is as consequential as model architecture for real-world performance. Second,
a single classification score is insufficient for evaluating a field-deployed system, because
the gap between laboratory and field performance is often larger than the gap between competing
model architectures.

\subsubsection{Existing mobile disease-detection systems}

Several mobile applications have attempted to deliver deep-learning-based plant disease diagnosis
to farmers. Plantix, developed by PEAT, uses a convolutional neural network to classify disease,
nutrient deficiency, and pest damage from photographs, with treatment advice delivered in
multiple languages \parencite{petrellaPlantix2023}. PlantVillage Nuru, developed by Penn State
University in partnership with IITA and the FAO, provides on-device inference using TensorFlow
Lite and was specifically designed for offline use---a critical feature for farmers in remote
areas with unreliable connectivity \parencite{mrishoPlantVillageNuru2020}.

\ChapterSubsection{Object Detection in Agricultural Vision}

\subsubsection{The YOLO detector family}

Object detection extends beyond classification by localizing \emph{where} relevant structures
appear within an image, not only \emph{what} they are. In agricultural contexts, detection is
useful because disease symptoms and plant structures often occupy small or partial regions of
a complex field image. A detector can localize leaves, panicles, fruits, ears, and other
crop parts that provide spatial context for downstream diagnosis reasoning.

The YOLO (You Only Look Once) family has become the dominant real-time detector in agricultural
applications because it balances inference speed with competitive accuracy
\parencite{jiangYOLOSurvey2022}. YOLOv8, released by Ultralytics, supports detection,
segmentation, classification, and pose estimation across multiple model sizes, with streamlined
training, validation, and export workflows \parencite{ultralyticsYolov8}. Recent studies
applying YOLO variants to agricultural tasks report mAP@50 values ranging from 0.70 to 0.92
depending on crop type, dataset size, and whether evaluation uses controlled or field images
\parencite{tian2020AppleFruitYOLO,zhengSmallObjDetAgri2023}.

Krishi Vaidya selects YOLOv8 nano (\texttt{yolov8n}) for the plant-part detection role. The
``nano'' variant was chosen deliberately: with approximately 3.2 million parameters, it is
the smallest YOLOv8 architecture and thus the most feasible for mobile deployment without
requiring server-side inference. The detector's role in the system is structural rather than
diagnostic---it identifies plant parts, not diseases. This separation is architecturally
important because it prevents conflation of detection quality with diagnostic accuracy in
the evaluation.

\subsubsection{Detection evaluation methodology}

The object-detection evaluation methodology used in Krishi Vaidya follows established protocols.
The PASCAL VOC challenge introduced the convention of evaluating detections at an intersection-over-union (IoU) threshold of 0.50 \parencite{everinghamPASCALVOC2010}. The
COCO benchmark extended this to a more stringent protocol, averaging average precision across
IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95, which rewards precise localization rather than only correct
object identification \parencite{linCOCO2014}. A comparative analysis of detection metrics
further clarifies that precision, recall, F1-score, and mAP measure complementary aspects of
detector behavior and should be reported together \parencite{padillaMetricsSurvey2021}.

Krishi Vaidya reports mAP@50, mAP@50-95, precision, recall, and F1-score because each captures
a different failure mode. High precision with low recall indicates excessive conservatism;
high recall with low precision indicates noisy detections. For a first-stage detector whose
outputs feed into a VLM pipeline, recall is arguably more important than precision, because
missed plant regions cannot be analyzed downstream, while false detections can be filtered by
subsequent processing. The threshold analysis in Chapter 4 examines this tradeoff explicitly.

\subsubsection{Small-object and dense-detection challenges}

A persistent challenge in agricultural object detection is the detection of small, clustered,
or partially occluded objects \parencite{zhengSmallObjDetAgri2023}. Rice panicles and grain
clusters, two classes in the Krishi Vaidya dataset, are frequently small, densely packed, and
visually similar to surrounding foliage. Prior work on small-object detection in agriculture
identifies three compounding difficulties: limited feature representation at low spatial
resolutions, annotation noise from ambiguous bounding-box boundaries, and extreme class
imbalance when small-object classes are mixed with large-object classes in the same dataset.

The Krishi Vaidya YOLO results (Chapter 4) confirm that these challenges are present in the
current dataset. The detector achieves low recall for rice panicle and brassica leaf classes---a
pattern consistent with the small-object detection literature rather than with a general model
deficiency.

\subsubsection{Quantization and deployment}

Deploying trained models to resource-constrained environments requires balancing accuracy
against inference speed, memory footprint, and hardware compatibility. Post-training quantization
compresses model weights from full-precision floating-point (FP32) to reduced formats such as
FP16 or INT8, reducing model size at the cost of potential accuracy degradation
\parencite{garg2022quantizationSurvey}. Ultralytics provides export paths for ONNX, TensorFlow
Lite, and INT8 quantization \parencite{ultralyticsExport}, which Krishi Vaidya uses to prepare
deployment variants.

The deployment decision is more nuanced than simply selecting the smallest model. As the
per-class analysis in Chapter 4 demonstrates, quantization affects different classes unevenly:
some classes improve slightly under INT8, while others lose performance. A deployment-oriented
evaluation must therefore examine per-class behavior alongside aggregate metrics, a point
that the broader deployment literature emphasizes when discussing quantization tradeoffs for
domain-specific applications \parencite{paleyes2022MLSystemsChallenges}.

\ChapterSubsection{Vision-Language Models for Structured Diagnosis}

\subsubsection{From classification to structured reasoning}

Vision-language models represent a qualitative shift in how crop disease diagnosis can be
framed. Traditional approaches produce a class label; VLMs can produce structured text
containing disease name, confidence, severity, symptom description, and advisory content
from a single image-and-prompt input. This capability aligns with the decision-support
framing of diagnosis: a farmer needs an actionable response, not only a label.

The foundational architecture for visual instruction tuning was established by LLaVA, which
demonstrated that a vision encoder could be connected to a language model and fine-tuned on
visual instruction-following data \parencite{liuLLaVA2023}. Subsequent work has expanded this
paradigm across multiple model families, with surveys documenting the rapid growth of VLMs
for vision tasks including visual question answering, image captioning, and structured extraction
\parencite{zhangVLMSurvey2024}.

In the agricultural domain specifically, Agri-LLaVA introduced a knowledge-infused multimodal
model covering more than 200 pest and disease types, using a two-phase training strategy
of feature alignment followed by instruction tuning \parencite{wangAgriLLaVA2024}. The
LLMI-CDP system similarly demonstrated that LoRA-adapted VLMs can achieve high identification
accuracy for crop diseases when fine-tuned on domain-specific data with constrained output
formats \parencite{yangLLMCropDisease2024}. These results are relevant to Krishi Vaidya's
VLM design, which also uses LoRA adaptation on a pre-trained VLM (Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct)
for disease-oriented structured output.

\subsubsection{Parameter-efficient fine-tuning}

Full fine-tuning of a multi-billion-parameter VLM requires computational resources beyond the
reach of most student and research environments. LoRA addresses this by freezing the base model
weights and training low-rank adapter matrices that are injected into selected attention and
feed-forward layers \parencite{hu2021lora}. This reduces the number of trainable parameters by
orders of magnitude while preserving the base model's learned representations.

QLoRA extends this approach by loading the frozen model weights in 4-bit quantized form,
enabling fine-tuning of models that would otherwise exceed available GPU memory
\parencite{dettmers2023qlora}. Krishi Vaidya combines both techniques: the Qwen2.5-VL base
model is loaded in 4-bit NF4 quantization, and LoRA adapters are trained on the target modules.
This configuration allowed fine-tuning on a single NVIDIA L4 GPU with 22 GB memory---a
practical constraint that shapes the entire VLM methodology.

The distinction between training feasibility and diagnostic reliability is important. LoRA and
QLoRA make it \emph{possible} to fine-tune a VLM on limited hardware; they do not guarantee that
the resulting model will produce reliable structured outputs. The gap between training loss and
practical parseability---a central finding of this manuscript---is consistent with observations
in the broader transfer-learning literature, where distribution shift between training
supervision and free-generation inference remains a persistent challenge
\parencite{zhuangTransferLearningSurvey2021}.

\subsubsection{Structured output and parseability}

For a farmer-facing advisory system, model output must be machine-parseable. A natural-language
response that contains useful information but cannot be reliably converted into structured
application fields (disease name, confidence, treatment steps) is difficult to store, validate,
or display consistently. Krishi Vaidya evaluates VLM outputs not only by classification
accuracy but also by parseability: whether the generated text can be extracted into a canonical
disease label for downstream use.

This emphasis on parseability distinguishes the evaluation approach from prior work that
reports only top-1 or top-5 classification accuracy. When a VLM produces free-form text,
the space of possible outputs is vastly larger than a fixed label set, and a model may
generate semantically correct but syntactically unparseable responses. The 67.7 percent
parse-error rate observed in the Krishi Vaidya VLM evaluation (Chapter 4) highlights this
challenge and motivates future work on constrained decoding and output-format supervision.

\ChapterSubsection{Offline-First Mobile Architecture}

Designing mobile applications for agricultural field use in developing countries requires
confronting a fundamental infrastructure constraint: connectivity is intermittent, and the
application must remain useful when the network is unavailable. The ``local-first'' software
paradigm, articulated by \textcite{kleppmannLocalFirst2019}, establishes seven design
ideals---including ``network optional,'' ``no spinners,'' and ``your work is not hostage''---that
prioritize local data ownership and offline functionality over cloud-dependent architectures.

In practice, offline-first mobile applications use an embedded local database (typically SQLite
on mobile platforms) as the primary data store, with server synchronization occurring
opportunistically in the background \parencite{mehdiOfflineFirstMobile2022}. This architecture
inverts the conventional assumption that the server is the source of truth; instead, the device
holds authoritative local state, and the server reconciles differences when connectivity permits.

Krishi Vaidya adopts this pattern. The mobile application stores scan records, crop plots,
tasks, notes, advisories, settings, and synchronization queue entries in SQLite. When the
device is online, scans are submitted to the backend for VLM-based diagnosis; when offline,
scans are queued locally and the application attempts to return cached advisory content. This
design is aligned with the documented connectivity challenges facing Nepalese rural farmers
\parencite{khatriNepalDigitalAgri2023,faoNepalDigitalAgriculture} and with the broader
literature on mobile systems for resource-constrained environments
\parencite{silvestriSmallholderTechAdoption2021}.

\ChapterSubsection{Backend Architecture and ML System Design}

The backend layer of a machine-learning-integrated application serves a role that is often
underemphasized in model-centric research: it transforms raw model output into a validated,
structured, application-facing response. This responsibility includes request validation,
authentication, model-service orchestration, output parsing, advisory generation, record
persistence, and error handling.

The challenges of deploying machine learning in production software systems are well documented.
\textcite{sculleyMLTechDebt2015} introduced the concept of ``hidden technical debt'' in ML
systems, arguing that the machine learning code itself is a small fraction of a production
system; the surrounding infrastructure for data collection, feature extraction, serving,
monitoring, and configuration management constitutes the majority of the engineering effort.
A more recent survey confirms that challenges in deploying ML systems span data management,
model training, deployment, monitoring, and organizational concerns
\parencite{paleyes2022MLSystemsChallenges}.

Krishi Vaidya's backend addresses several of these challenges explicitly. The Express/Mongoose
API separates concerns into middleware, controllers, services, repositories, and models. The
diagnosis pipeline validates uploaded images before calling the VLM service, parses VLM output
defensively, generates structured advisory content through a schema-validated secondary model
call, and returns a response with confidence tiers and uncertainty flags. This layered design
reflects the principle that model outputs should not reach users unvalidated---a principle that
the ML systems literature identifies as critical for trustworthy deployment
\parencite{paleyes2022MLSystemsChallenges}.

\ChapterSubsection{Research Gap and Positioning}

The reviewed literature reveals three specific gaps that Krishi Vaidya addresses at the scale
of a final-year undergraduate project.

\textbf{Gap 1: Integration gap.} The majority of published plant-disease detection systems
terminate at a classification output. They do not implement the surrounding infrastructure
required for a farmer to capture an image, receive structured advice, store a diagnosis record,
manage crop plots, or revisit historical scans. Existing mobile applications such as Plantix and
PlantVillage Nuru address portions of this workflow but do not combine object detection, VLM-based
interpretation, backend orchestration, and offline-aware mobile delivery within a single
integrated system
\parencite{petrellaPlantix2023,mrishoPlantVillageNuru2020,mohanty2016plantvillage}.

\textbf{Gap 2: Structured-output evaluation gap.} Most VLM and LLM evaluations in agriculture
report classification accuracy but do not evaluate whether model outputs can be reliably parsed
into machine-usable fields \parencite{wangAgriLLaVA2024,yangLLMCropDisease2024}. For a system
that must display structured advisory content in a mobile application, parseability is as
important as semantic correctness. Krishi Vaidya introduces parseability as an explicit
evaluation metric alongside conventional classification accuracy.

\textbf{Gap 3: Contextual deployment gap.} Published benchmarks for plant disease detection
rarely address the deployment constraints of the target context: offline operation, bilingual
output, per-class quantization behavior, mobile-device inference, and integration with
farmer-facing record-keeping workflows \parencite{thakurPlantDiseaseFieldGap2022}. Krishi
Vaidya documents these deployment-oriented concerns as integral parts of the system evaluation
rather than treating them as engineering afterthoughts.

Krishi Vaidya does not claim to resolve these gaps completely. It is a student-built prototype,
and its VLM prediction results (Chapter 4) demonstrate that structured diagnostic reliability
remains a substantial open challenge. The contribution is the integration itself---demonstrating
how trained model components, a validated backend, and an offline-aware mobile application can be
composed into a diagnosis-support workflow---and the honest reporting of where this integration
currently succeeds and where it falls short.
